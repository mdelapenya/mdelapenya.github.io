In this post I'm transcribing some thoughts I wrote a few days ago. I hope someone can get something out of them:

"Well, here I am at the keyboard again, hoping that these lines turn out to be 100% true.

I'm writing while I wait for the last grade of the last subject on my university record, in the Ingeniería Técnica en Informática de Gestión degree (a technical engineering degree in business computing).

Six years ago I enrolled at UNED (Spain's national distance-learning university), looking to finish the degree I started in 2001 at a regular on-campus university. I say "finish" as a euphemism, because I arrived at UNED with 4 subjects already credited, and from there to 181 credits I could well say I did the whole degree (28 subjects) at a distance.

I enrolled without knowing that some hard years were coming: years of sacrifice, upsets, exhaustion and everything bad you can imagine when you decide to combine an engineering degree with a day job. At first I worked for the regional government of Castilla-La Mancha but, restless as I am, I wanted to move forward and jumped to consulting firms in Madrid, going through several projects and many clients. So, given the state of the industry in Spain, imagine the wave-particle duality of work and study.

I have to say I didn't think about any of that. My goal was always the end. I never thought about the hours of dedication, never. Year after year I enrolled, subtracting the number of credits I had left, and all I wanted was to reach this exact place, where your stomach ties itself in knots from the nerves of waiting and cold sweat runs down your temples. This exact moment when you reap what you sowed, full of hope, to see that the digits hidden on the university website are strictly greater than 4.99999.

I think about everything I learned and I see it with different eyes. Maybe because I combined study and work, I saw the usefulness of each subject more easily. Many times I found an immediate application of what I had learned in my professional environment, and many other times I sensed that it was useless or out of date... _Why is anyone still teaching programming in MODULA-2? Here is a big underlying problem of Spanish degrees: when you are at university, you have no idea what impact the knowledge of many subjects will have on your day-to-day professional life. I don't know if it's the system, which struggles to open its doors and look further, bringing the business world closer to the university, or if it's us students, who are busy with other things. A bit of both, right?_

What matters is that, whatever the answer to that why, I felt comfortable along the way. I tried to take on each subject with **enthusiasm**, and I studied imagining that I would apply the material the next day. I don't think I ever got overwhelmed, and without a doubt that made it easier to get through the hard part of the road.

I look back and I have to confess that I have really been lucky. Lucky to work for the Public Administration, where they let me learn to make decisions and take on responsibilities. Lucky to have gone through the Spanish meat-industry projects with clients who respected working hours. Lucky to have had project managers who didn't give me much trouble (although I saw some unpleasant situations). Lucky to have had teammates who helped me adapt to those wild projects... Lucky to know what I wanted, and to have met people in whom I could see myself (yes, Jorge, I'm talking about you, I always had you in mind as an example).

Lucky also to have been born into a family that always let me do my own thing, that **knew how to watch me explore, doubt and make mistakes**, and gave me the autonomy I needed to take on adventures like this one.

And lucky to have arrived at a company like **Liferay**, that 21st-century company that @RCarpintier will reveal to us at the next Symposium. I have found a place for **professional and personal growth** that I couldn't have imagined before. I have to thank my colleagues deeply for making my day-to-day at the office so easy... the credit is yours too, for creating such a healthy climate of excellence!!

But what makes me feel most fortunate is having by my side a person who, despite all the difficulties, was always there: in the celebrations when I passed, in the sacrifices of the study days, in the nerves before the exam (theirs) and in the nerves of the wait (mine). A part of this credit is yours, for being so actively with me on this long road.

And now I wonder... what comes next? I don't know. The grade isn't out yet, but I've already started looking into master's programs...

I'm convinced it can't be any other way!!"
